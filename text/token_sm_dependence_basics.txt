--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
0. THE QUESTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Everything the project currently knows about whether satellite imagery matters
is mediated by a TRAINED NETWORK. Section 24 of the runbook perturbed the inputs
of a checkpoint and concluded the tokens matter. Section 24.12 then showed the
content is largely a SITE FINGERPRINT, and that the fingerprint share grows with
depth (the temporal share of the effect was 39% / 27% / 12% at 0-10 / 10-30 /
30-100 cm).

A sceptic can answer all of that with one sentence:

    "Your model responds to the tokens. That says nothing about whether the
     tokens contain soil moisture."

This document describes how to answer the question with NO TRAINED MODEL
ANYWHERE. Nothing is fitted to predict soil moisture from tokens. The evidence
is a dependence statistic measured against an explicit null.

What we are asking:

    For each day t we have an image embedding x_t (768 numbers) and a soil
    moisture value y_t. Does knowing x tell you anything about y?

    Formally:   dependence  <=>  P(y | x) != P(y)
                independence <=> the joint distribution factorises

What we are NOT asking, and must not later pretend we asked:

    - not "can we predict soil moisture from imagery" (that is a modelling
      question, and a negative answer there could just mean a bad model)
    - not "does the imagery CAUSE the moisture reading"
    - not "does the embedding contain moisture information that is not already
      in ERA5 and the calendar" -- that is a harder, conditional question,
      discussed in section 7


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. BUILDING UP FROM THE EASY CASE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1.1 If the embedding were a single number
-----------------------------------------
Suppose x were one number -- say Sentinel-1 VV backscatter in dB. You would plot
y against x, compute a Pearson correlation, get r = 0.4, and ask "is that real?"

Two things go wrong immediately. Both survive into the 768-dimensional case, so
they are worth stating carefully now.

  PROBLEM 1 -- Pearson only sees straight lines.
      If y were a perfect U-shape in x, Pearson r would be near zero while the
      dependence is total. A test that can only see linear relationships will
      report "nothing" for a large class of real relationships.

  PROBLEM 2 -- the textbook p-value assumes independent observations.
      The standard significance test for r assumes your n observations are
      independent draws. Soil moisture on consecutive days is very nearly the
      same number. With 300 daily values you do not have 300 independent
      observations; you have something closer to a few dozen. Every classical
      p-value computed on such data is too small, often by a lot.


1.2 The idea that fixes assumption-free testing: shuffle it yourself
--------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the entire foundation of the method. It needs no formulas.

You have 300 correctly-paired days. Compute your statistic -- anything you like.
Say it comes out 0.4.

Now SHUFFLE the y values and recompute. You have personally destroyed any
relationship, so whatever number you get is what "no relationship" looks like.
Do it 999 times. You now have 999 numbers describing a world where nothing is
going on. Say they spread from -0.12 to +0.11.

Your real 0.4 sits far outside that spread. A relationship that strong
essentially never arises from a random pairing.

    p-value = (1 + how many of the 999 fakes reached 0.4) / 1000

WHY THIS IS VALID: if there truly is no relationship, then the real pairing is
nothing special -- it is one arrangement among many equally likely ones. So the
real number is itself just another draw from that same histogram, and it lands
in the top 5% exactly 5% of the time. That is the whole argument.

No normality. No independence assumption. No large-sample approximation. And it
works for ANY statistic, however strange.

    CONSEQUENCE, and it is important:
    the choice of statistic controls POWER (how often a true effect is found).
    It cannot control VALIDITY. A poor statistic finds less; it cannot lie.

This is why later concerns about the distance-correlation estimator being biased
in high dimensions are a REPORTING problem, not an inference problem: the fake
draws carry the identical bias, and it cancels in the p-value.


1.3 The catch, and it is the one that matters here
---------------------------------------------------
The shuffle must destroy ONLY the thing being tested.

Soil moisture is smooth in time -- today's value is close to yesterday's.
Embeddings are smooth too, because consecutive acquisitions see nearly the same
scene. When you shuffle y at random, you destroy the pairing AND you destroy the
smoothness.

So the 999 fake datasets are jagged while the real one is smooth. That is not a
fair comparison. Two smooth series drift into apparent agreement far more often
than two jagged ones do. (This is the classic spurious-regression effect: two
INDEPENDENT random walks routinely show large correlations.)

The consequence is severe and specific:

    the fake histogram comes out too narrow
    -> the real value looks impressively extreme
    -> you announce a discovery from pure noise

THE FIX: shift instead of shuffle. Rotate y in time by a random number of days,
wrapping the end around to the start ("circular" or "toroidal" shift). The
series stays exactly as smooth as it really is; only the date alignment is
wrong. Now the fakes match the real data in every respect except the single
thing under test.

This is standard practice in spatial ecology for exactly this reason. It is
approximately exact for a stationary series and exactly correct for a
circularly-stationary one.

    THIS IS THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT DESIGN DECISION IN THE WHOLE METHOD.
    Get it wrong and every result downstream is a false positive.

THIS IS NOT A THEORETICAL WORRY. MEASURED 2026-08-11: two INDEPENDENT AR(1)
series, n = 300, 400 repetitions, 199 null draws, statistic = |Pearson r|. The
truth is that there is no relationship, so a correct test must reject 5% of the
time and no more.

    autocorrelation      shuffle        circular shift     (correct = 5.0%)
    rho = 0.00             6.0%             6.8%
    rho = 0.70            23.0%             6.0%
    rho = 0.90            51.0%             6.5%
    rho = 0.95            66.5%             8.0%

Read the rho = 0.90 row. Shuffling declares a discovery on completely unrelated
data 51% of the time -- ten times the nominal rate. Daily soil moisture is at
least this autocorrelated. With no autocorrelation (rho = 0) the two nulls agree,
which confirms the effect is caused by smoothness and nothing else.

Circular shift stays near 5% throughout. It does drift slightly optimistic at
rho = 0.95 (8.0%, against a Monte-Carlo standard error of about 1.1%), which is
itself an argument for setting the minimum lag per station from the measured
autocorrelation rather than fixing it globally -- see 6(e).


1.4 Now the 768 dimensions
---------------------------
Why not just run 768 separate correlations, one per embedding coordinate, and
correct for multiple testing?

  The obvious objection: with 768 tests you expect ~38 to look significant at
  p<0.05 by chance alone. Bonferroni handles that but destroys power, and the
  signal may be spread thinly across many coordinates rather than concentrated
  in one.

  The deeper objection, and the decisive one: THE 768 NUMBERS ARE AN ARBITRARY
  COORDINATE SYSTEM. A transformer's residual stream has no privileged axes.
  Rotate the basis by any orthogonal matrix Q and the embedding X becomes XQ --
  identical information, but every single per-coordinate correlation changes.

  A statistic whose answer depends on the orientation of an arbitrary basis is
  measuring the basis, not the data.

So what IS invariant to rotation? DISTANCES BETWEEN POINTS. However you rotate
the space, the distance between day i's embedding and day j's embedding is
unchanged. Therefore the test must be built out of distances.


1.5 The intuition, in one sentence
-----------------------------------
    If the embedding knows about soil moisture, then days whose images look
    similar to each other should have similar soil moisture.

Made concrete: for every pair of days (i,j) compute

    a_ij = distance between the two embeddings
    b_ij = |SM_i - SM_j|

If there is dependence, small a should go with small b. "Distance correlation"
is a carefully normalised version of correlating a against b. The k-nearest-
neighbour version is the blunt form: take the 10 most similar images, and ask
whether their soil moisture values are closer to each other than random pairs
are.

Everything else -- characteristic functions, double-centring, bias correction --
is machinery that makes this intuition exact. It is not where the belief comes
from.

The formal property that justifies the choice: distance correlation is zero IF
AND ONLY IF the two variables are independent, in any dimensions (Szekely,
Rizzo & Bakirov 2007). Pearson gives zero for plenty of dependent pairs. This
matters most for a NEGATIVE result: a test consistent against all alternatives
lets "we found nothing" mean something.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WHAT GETS COMPARED -- THE THREE ARMS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Soil-moisture variance splits into a SPATIAL part (some places are wetter than
others) and a TEMPORAL part (some days are wetter than others at one place).
These are different physical questions, they fail independently, and pooling
them re-creates exactly the confound section 24.12 identified. So they are
tested separately and never merged.

  ARM T   (time)         same station, different dates
                         "do wet DAYS look different from dry days?"
                         main confound: season, rain, phenology

  ARM S-coarse (space)   different stations, far apart
                         "do wet PLACES look different from dry places?"
                         main confound: GEOGRAPHY -- nearby places look alike
                         and are similarly wet, so an uncontrolled result
                         proves only that embeddings encode location

  ARM S-fine (space)     different stations <5 km apart, SAME DATE
                         "at fixed weather and climate, does a difference in
                          embedding track a difference in soil moisture?"
                         main confound: land cover

ARM S-fine is the cleanest test available. Two stations a kilometre apart share
the same storm, the same season, the same climate -- every nuisance variable is
held fixed by construction, and what remains is spatial moisture contrast.

Its cost is sample size. Measured from csvs/station_splits.csv:

    890 stations with soil moisture, in 824 location groups
     31 groups contain >= 2 SM stations  ->   97 stations, 175 pairs
        105 pairs < 2.24 km  (25 groups)  <- both inside ONE 224x224 patch
        175 pairs < 5 km     (31 groups)
    networks: TxSON 28 pairs, SNOTEL 18, FMI 14, iRON 4, AmeriFlux 4

n = 31 clusters must be stated in every claim this arm supports, and aggregation
must be cluster-equal -- TxSON alone would otherwise carry the result.

4. WHY EACH BOX EXISTS -- AND WHAT BREAKS WITHOUT IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

[1] INPUT
    L12 is the final TerraMind layer, chosen by decision, not by evidence. It is
    the deepest and most semantic representation; if soil moisture survives
    anywhere it is most likely to be a high-level property there, but it is also
    the layer most likely to have discarded low-level radiometry. The shallower
    layers L3/L6/L9 exist on disk and are the obvious follow-up if L12 is null.
    WITHOUT IT: nothing. This is the given.

[2] REDUCE 196 PATCHES TO THE STATION FOOTPRINT
    Each acquisition is 196 patch tokens covering 2.24 x 2.24 km. The in-situ
    sensor is a point. Averaging the four tokens over the station (a ~320 m
    footprint) is the closest available match to what the sensor measures.
    WITHOUT IT: averaging all 196 tokens would mix in land 1 km away, which has
    its own moisture, diluting any real relationship. Using a single token would
    be arbitrary, because the station falls exactly on the corner between four.

[3] JOIN ON DATE, qc == 0 ONLY
    The label file contains gap-filled values flagged qc==1. Those are
    climatological interpolations, not observations -- they are a smooth function
    of day-of-year. Including them would inject pure seasonality into y and
    inflate any apparent relationship with a seasonal embedding.
    WITHOUT IT: a substantial fraction of the "signal" would be our own
    gap-filling algorithm being detected.

[4] SUBTRACT THE STATION MEAN
    This is the first half of the scientific argument. Across stations, the
    dominant variation in both embeddings and soil moisture is site identity: a
    desert looks different from a bog and is drier. A test on raw values would
    fire strongly and mean only "different places are different". Section 24.12
    already established that the fingerprint dominates.
    WITHOUT IT: guaranteed strong positive result carrying no information.

[5] REGRESS OUT DAY-OF-YEAR HARMONICS
    The second half of the scientific argument. Within a station, both the
    imagery (vegetation green-up, sun angle, snow) and the soil moisture follow
    an annual cycle. Any statistic on unadjusted series would detect "both things
    have a seasonal cycle", which is true and uninteresting.
    Removing k = 1..3 harmonics from BOTH sides leaves the question: beyond the
    seasonal norm at this site, do wet anomalies look different from dry ones?
    WITHOUT IT: a strong positive result that is just the calendar.
    NOTE: this is done symmetrically to both sides, so nothing crosses between X
    and y. That is what keeps the procedure model-free.

[6] Z-SCORE, THEN PCA TO ~20 DIMENSIONS
    Two separate reasons.
    (a) z-scoring: the 768 embedding coordinates have very different scales.
        Without standardising, a handful of high-variance coordinates would
        dictate every distance and the other 700 would be invisible.
    (b) PCA: in high dimensions all pairwise distances concentrate toward the
        same value -- the relative spread collapses and distance-based statistics
        lose their power. Reducing to ~20 components restores contrast.
    IS THIS CHEATING? No. PCA is computed from X alone; y never enters it. The
    null permutes only y, so the reduction is identical in the real and fake
    datasets and cannot manufacture a difference. It must be fitted ONCE, outside
    the permutation loop.
    WITHOUT IT: the test still valid, but with much reduced power -- a real effect
    could be missed.

[7] DISTANCES AND U-CENTRING
    Distances because they are the rotation-invariant summary (section 1.4).
    U-centring (rather than the simpler double-centring) because the naive
    estimator of distance correlation is biased UPWARD, and the bias grows with
    dimension: Szekely & Rizzo (2013) showed it tends to 1 as dimension grows for
    ANY data, related or not. At 768 dimensions, pure noise can return ~0.9.
    WITHOUT IT: the p-value would still be correct (the bias cancels between real
    and fake), but any reported effect size would be meaningless. The rule is
    therefore: never quote an effect size from the biased estimator. This is the
    same class of error as quoting mean NSE (runbook section 22).

[8] CIRCULAR SHIFT, NOT SHUFFLE
    See section 1.3. This is the load-bearing step.
    The minimum lag of 30 days exists so the shifted series is not still aligned
    with itself through autocorrelation.
    WITHOUT IT: systematic false positives; the entire study would be wrong.

[9] THE PER-STATION p-VALUE
    The (1 + count)/(B + 1) form, rather than count/B, is what makes the test
    exactly valid rather than approximately so, and prevents a p-value of zero.

[10]-[11] THE FRACTION OF STATIONS
    One station with ~300 autocorrelated days is weak evidence either way. The
    strength of the design comes from ~890 near-independent repetitions of the
    same experiment. Under a true null, exactly 5% of stations should reach
    p<=0.05 -- that is what a p-value means. The scientific question becomes:
    is it 5%, or is it 40%?

[12] THE GLOBAL NULL
    Stations in the same cluster are not independent, so a binomial test on the
    fraction from [11] would overstate significance. Instead, shift y at every
    station simultaneously and recompute the whole aggregate. Any cross-station
    dependence is present in both the real and the fake numbers and cancels. This
    requires no independence assumption at all.
    WITHOUT IT: an over-confident global p-value.

[13] THE VERDICT, AND WHY IT IS CONDITIONAL
    A positive result is the START of the argument, not the end -- see section 5.
    A negative result is meaningless without the detection floor from Part C.

PART C GATES
    POSITIVE CONTROL: replace the target with raw Sentinel-1 VV backscatter from
    the same scene. The tokens were computed from exactly that image, so
    dependence MUST be strong. If it is not, the machinery is broken and every
    null elsewhere is a false negative. This mirrors the ERA5 positive control in
    runbook section 24.5, which is the reason section 24's result was
    interpretable at all.

    NULL CALIBRATION: with a shifted target, the fraction of stations at p<=0.05
    must come out near 5%. Materially higher means the minimum lag is too short
    for that site's autocorrelation, and the null must be fixed before anything
    else is believed.

    DETECTION FLOOR: plant a known signal of size alpha into real embeddings and
    find the smallest alpha the machine reliably recovers. This converts a null
    from the uninterpretable "we found nothing" into the scientific statement
    "there is no dependence above an effect of size X". A test with no power
    finds nothing by construction; without this step we could not tell the two
    apart.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
5. WHAT EACH OUTCOME IS ALLOWED TO CLAIM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

IF THE TEST FIRES:
    Allowed:     "the embedding co-varies with soil moisture, beyond site
                  identity and beyond the seasonal cycle"
    NOT allowed: "the embedding contains soil-moisture information"

    The gap between those two is not closed by any statistic. It is closed --
    partially -- by the design: subtracting the station mean removes site
    identity, removing harmonics removes season, and comparing two stations 1 km
    apart on the same day removes weather because they share it. Each of those is
    a PHYSICAL argument. The statistics only certify that what remains is not
    chance.

    So the honest place to be sceptical is not the mathematics. It is whether
    boxes [4] and [5] really remove what they claim to remove.

IF THE TEST DOES NOT FIRE:
    Allowed:     "no dependence above an effect of size X" (X from the detection
                  floor)
    NOT allowed: "the embeddings do not carry soil moisture"

    And a null at L12 immediately raises the next question rather than closing
    the topic: is the signal present in the shallower layers L3/L6/L9, which the
    U-Net decoder consumes directly? Those bundles are already on disk.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
6. THE ATTACK SURFACE -- WHERE THIS METHOD IS WEAKEST
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Stated by us rather than left for a reviewer to find.

(a) BOXES [4] AND [5] CARRY THE ENTIRE SCIENTIFIC ARGUMENT.
    Everything downstream is bookkeeping. If those two subtractions do not remove
    climate and season, a positive result is a confound and no amount of
    permutation testing repairs it.

(b) THREE HARMONICS UNDER-FIT REAL SEASONALITY.  <- attack this first
    Three sine/cosine pairs describe a SMOOTH annual cycle. Soil moisture
    seasonality is not smooth: snowmelt is a step, monsoon onset is a step. What
    the harmonics fail to remove stays in BOTH X and y, and shared leftover
    seasonality manufactures exactly the dependence we are looking for. The
    number 3 is a guess. Sensitivity to it must be reported, and a step-tolerant
    alternative (e.g. a per-station day-of-year climatology, or a smoothing
    spline) should be tested.

(c) SUPPORT MISMATCH -- 320 m token footprint versus a point sensor.
    The sensor integrates a few cubic centimetres of soil. The embedding sees
    ~320 m. That mismatch is real physical noise in y relative to X, and no
    statistic removes it. It can only push results toward a null -- which means a
    small measured effect might be genuine, and it is another reason the
    detection floor is mandatory.

(d) THE PCA DIMENSION ~20 IS ARBITRARY -- AND PCA CAN DELETE THE SIGNAL.
    If the answer moves with the number of components, there is no answer. The
    number must be swept and reported, not chosen.

    Worse, and discovered by accident on 2026-08-11: a pilot simulation planted a
    known signal along ONE embedding coordinate, then applied the same z-score +
    PCA-to-20 reduction described in box [6]. The distance test did NOT recover
    it. The reason is instructive and applies directly to the real method: PCA is
    UNSUPERVISED. It keeps the directions of largest variance, and there is no
    reason at all that the soil-moisture direction is among them. An unsupervised
    reduction can discard precisely the axis of interest.

    That pilot was mis-specified as a power benchmark and is NOT evidence about
    the real method -- its planted signal was one of 768 mutually independent
    directions, which is an unrealistically hard case, and its per-coordinate
    comparison used classical p-values, which fail outright on autocorrelated
    data (100% false positives -- itself an unintended demonstration of section
    1.1, Problem 2). But the MECHANISM it exposed is real and unresolved.

    Options, none yet chosen: sweep the component count; run the test on all 768
    dimensions with no reduction and accept the loss of power to distance
    concentration; or use a reduction that is not variance-based. This must be
    settled before the method is trusted.

(e) THE >= 30-DAY MINIMUM LAG ASSUMES SM DECORRELATES WITHIN A MONTH.
    At snow-dominated sites (SNOTEL is roughly half the evaluation pool) it does
    not. There the null stays too easy and false positives return. The lag should
    be set per station from the measured autocorrelation, not fixed globally.

(f) BOX [11] PRESUMES p-VALUES ARE UNIFORM UNDER THE NULL.
    That is precisely what the calibration gate must ESTABLISH, not assume. If
    calibration fails, [11] and [12] are both invalid.

(g) MULTIPLE COMPARISONS ACROSS THE STUDY.
    Three arms x several modality configurations x three depths x several
    choices of k is a large search space. One primary endpoint must be fixed IN
    WRITING before anything runs; everything else is explicitly exploratory.

(h) DEPENDENCE IS MARGINAL, NOT CONDITIONAL.
    Even a valid rejection does not show the embedding adds anything beyond ERA5
    and the calendar. Partial distance correlation can condition on those, but
    Shah & Peters (2020) proved no conditional-independence test controls error
    against all alternatives while keeping power. Conditioning reduces a
    confound; it does not eliminate one.

(i) THE CLOUD-SURVIVAL BIAS AFFECTS SENTINEL-2 ONLY.
    36% of S2 acquisitions were removed by cloud filtering, and clear days are
    dry-biased -- the surviving sample is not a random sample of days. Sentinel-1
    sees through cloud and has no such selection, which is one reason it is the
    primary modality. The size of the S2 bias should be measured directly by
    comparing the SM distribution on acquisition days against all days.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
7. OPEN DECISIONS -- NOT YET SETTLED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

1. Primary endpoint: distance correlation on continuous soil moisture, or the
   simpler and more intuitive two-sample version (do the embeddings of the
   wettest third of days differ from those of the driest third)? The second is
   easier to defend and to check by eye; the first uses every observation and has
   more power. Whichever is chosen must be declared before running.

2. Number of DOY harmonics, or a switch to a step-tolerant seasonal model --
   see 6(b).

3. Whether to run the null-calibration simulation on real embeddings before
   building anything (recommended: yes; it is cheap, and if the circular-shift
   null does not calibrate, the design needs rethinking rather than executing).
   The synthetic version above already settled the shuffle-versus-shift question;
   what remains is confirming it on the actual token time series, whose
   autocorrelation structure is not AR(1).

3b. A CORRECTED POWER AND DETECTION-FLOOR SIMULATION IS STILL OWED. The pilot run
   on 2026-08-11 was mis-specified -- see 6(d) -- so the method currently has NO
   measured power. Until it does, a negative result from this design would be
   uninterpretable. The corrected version must plant the signal along a realistic
   direction (a leading principal component of real embeddings, not an arbitrary
   coordinate of synthetic noise) and must resolve the box [6] reduction question
   at the same time.

4. Modality order. Proposal: Sentinel-1 + Sentinel-2 together first, since that
   is the honest "does the satellite branch carry it" question and has the most
   power; only then one modality at a time. DEM and LULC embeddings are static
   (one per station, no time axis), so they can contribute only to the spatial
   arms, never to the temporal one.

5. A separate, much cheaper question that needs none of this machinery: for the
   105 station pairs closer than 2.24 km, does the TRAINED model already get the
   spatial contrast right? Both stations of every pair fall in the same data
   split (location group was the cross-validation group), and 61 pairs with
   133,583 paired station-day-depth rows sit in the novel-station OOS split.
   That test needs no inference and no tokens -- only the prediction files
   already on disk -- and it answers a different and directly useful question:
   does the model know which of two nearby sites is wetter?
